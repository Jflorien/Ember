01 · Product shape

Two products in the market. Three surfaces in the codebase. One engine underneath.

You asked to split this into a DM product and a player product. That split is right commercially — they're bought by different people for different reasons — but it would be a mistake to split the code. During a live session these surfaces must agree on state within milliseconds. Two codebases means two implementations of the hardest part of the system, drifting apart. So: market two, build one, and let the third surface (the table) be the thing nobody else has.

Surface 1 · sold as the DM product

The DM Console

Desktop. Narrative control, initiative, hidden monster stats, encounter staging, fog reveal, undo. The only surface that can write authoritative events by hand.

/dm · desktop-first · ~55% of app surface area
Surface 2 · sold as the player product

The Player App

Phone and tablet. Character sheet, inventory, spell slots, dice, conditions, push-to-talk. Reads only what that player is allowed to see.

/play · mobile-first · ~30% of app surface area
Surface 3 · the differentiator, free

The Table View

Cast to a TV or projector. No chrome, no input. Renders the committed event stream as a living map — spells, fire, destruction, movement.

/table · read-only · ~15% of app surface area

The strategic read

The AI-dungeon-master category is already crowded, and almost all of it is a chat window that narrates a story to one person. The shared physical table — four friends, one screen on the wall, a fireball everyone sees at the same moment — is where nobody serious is competing.

That argues for the table view being free and viral rather than a premium tier. It is the screenshot that sells the product, and it's the surface a stranger sees first.

02 · Who it's for

Four jobs, in the order they should be solved.

These are ranked by how well you can validate them yourself. Persona 1 is you, which is why the private alpha is the right v1 — you are the only user you can observe continuously and for free.

Primary · validates fastest

The player with no DM

Loves the idea of the game, has never played, because running one is a real job and nobody in the group volunteers. Needs the AI DM to exist before the product means anything to them.

Job: "Let me actually play tonight, without recruiting a DM."

Highest willingness to pay

The overloaded DM

Already runs a table. Spends hours on prep, juggles a laptop, notes, and three tabs during play. Will pay for time back and for the table to look extraordinary.

Job: "Cut my prep, and give my players something to gasp at."

Volume, not revenue

The player at an existing table

Wants a sheet that does the arithmetic, dice that can't be fudged, and to stop asking "wait, how much HP do I have?". Arrives via their DM. Should never need to pay.

Job: "Keep my character straight so I can pay attention to the story."

Deferred — do not build for this yet

The solo player

One human, AI DM, no group. Tempting because it's the easiest to serve technically, but it puts you head-on against the crowded AI-fiction category and abandons your actual moat.

Job: "Give me a campaign I can play alone."

03 · Architecture spine

Everything in the requirements map hangs off this one contract.

The prototype already proved it: nothing calls the renderer directly. The DM — human or model — proposes events, a deterministic rules engine validates them, and only committed events reach any surface. Get this right and every feature below becomes an independent, testable unit of work. Get it wrong and you rewrite the product at month four.

// The one contract. Everything else is a subscriber.
type GameEvent = {
  id:        string        // ULID, monotonic — this is also the ordering key
  session_id:string
  seq:       number        // gap-free per session; clients detect drops
  type:      'cast' | 'attack' | 'damage' | 'heal' | 'move'
           | 'condition' | 'terrain' | 'destroy' | 'death'
           | 'reveal' | 'loot' | 'narration' | 'round'
  actor:     string | null
  payload:   Json          // zod-validated per type, versioned
  visibility:'public' | 'dm_only' | 'player:<id>'   // enforced in Postgres RLS, never in the UI
  proposed_by:'human' | 'model'
  committed_at:timestamptz
}

Four rules that must never be broken

  • The model never holds game state. Postgres does. The model receives a serialised snapshot per turn and proposes mutations. HP that lives in a context window will drift within twenty minutes of play.
  • Hidden information never leaves the server. If the boss has 140 HP remaining, that number must not be in the player client's memory and hidden with CSS. Enforce with row-level security on visibility. This is the single most common way products like this leak.
  • The event log is append-only. Undo is a compensating event, never a delete. This gives you session replay, the recap feature, and debugging, all for free.
  • Dice are rolled server-side. Seeded, logged, auditable. A player who suspects the dice are lying stops trusting the product entirely.

Character sheets are jsonb deliberately. The 5e sheet is deeply nested and will change shape a dozen times before you ship; normalising it now buys nothing and costs a migration every week. Normalise later, if ever.

05 · Non-functional requirements

The constraints that decide whether it feels like a product or a demo.

Latency

Event commit → visible on every surface: <150ms p95. AI DM text response: <2.5s to first token. Voice, when it exists: <2s to first audio. Past roughly three seconds a table stops waiting and starts talking over it.

Table view performance

60fps at 1080p on a five-year-old laptop driving a TV. This caps particle counts and rules out per-frame generative anything. Budget it as a hard constraint, not an aspiration.

Reconnection

A phone that sleeps mid-session must resync from last_seq and catch up without a reload. Sessions run four hours; every device will drop at least once.

Information security

RLS policies on every table. Player clients must be provably unable to fetch dm_only rows. Worth an automated test that logs in as a player and asserts the boss's HP is unreachable.

Privacy & jurisdiction

You're in the EU: GDPR applies from user one. Export and delete-account must exist before any public signup. Voice makes this heavier — recordings are personal data, so transcribe and discard rather than store.

Age

This hobby has a lot of minors. An AI generating unbounded narrative for under-13s is a problem you do not want to discover after launch. Age-gate at signup and decide the policy before beta, not after.

Cost per session

A four-hour AI-DM session with continuous narration is a real unit cost. Instrument tokens per session from the first AI commit, or you will price the product blind.

Accessibility

Sheets must be screen-reader navigable and the map must not encode meaning in colour alone — red/green token rings fail for a meaningful share of players. Cheap now, expensive to retrofit.

06 · Risk register

Ordered by what would actually kill the product.
1

The AI DM is competent but not fun

A model can adjudicate rules and produce serviceable prose. Whether it can hold narrative tension, remember that the innkeeper lied in session two, and react to a player doing something genuinely stupid — that is unproven, and it is the entire value proposition for your primary persona. This is not an engineering risk you can estimate away.

De-risk firstBefore building any UI for it, run a text-only AI DM through a real two-hour session with real friends over a chat window. If that session is boring, no amount of fireball VFX rescues the product, and you have learned it in a week rather than a quarter.
2

Scope is three products wearing a trenchcoat

A virtual tabletop, a rules engine, and an AI narrative system are each a multi-year product on their own. Solo, part-time, the honest failure mode here is eleven months of building with nothing playable.

De-risk firstEvery milestone below has an exit criterion that is "a real session happened." If a milestone can't produce a playable session, it is scoped wrong. Cut features until it can.
3

Model proposes illegal events, engine rejects, session stalls

Structured output gets you syntactically valid events. It does not get you legal ones — a spell without a slot, a target out of range, a creature acting twice. Reject-and-retry loops burn seconds, and seconds at a table are visible.

De-risk firstGive the model a pre-computed menu of legal actions in its snapshot rather than the raw rules. Constrain the space instead of validating after the fact. Measure the rejection rate from day one and treat it as a headline metric.
4

Hidden information leaks to player clients

The single most damaging bug class this product can have. One player noticing the boss's HP in a network tab ends the illusion permanently, and it is trivially easy to do accidentally when you're moving fast.

De-risk firstWrite the RLS policies and the adversarial test in P0, before there is any hidden data to leak. Retrofitting this is far harder than starting with it.
5

Content licensing beyond the SRD

SRD 5.2.1 under CC BY 4.0 covers the rules, and that's genuinely generous. It does not cover the D&D name, the iconic monsters, or published adventures — and importing from D&D Beyond is not a thing you can build on.

De-risk firstShip attribution correctly from the first commit, write original adventures, and run a trademark search on the product name before you buy a domain or print anything.
6

Nobody has a group to play with

Even a perfect product fails if a signup arrives alone and bounces. Your primary persona by definition does not have a functioning table.

De-risk laterThe AI DM is the answer to this — a solo or duo session needs no group. Worth noting that it makes the AI DM a distribution feature, not just a product feature.

The sequencing argument, stated plainly

P1 before P3 looks wrong for you personally — you don't have a DM, so a human-DM tabletop doesn't let you play. Build it anyway. P1 is where the rules engine, the sheets, and the event vocabulary get built, and the AI DM in P3 is only as good as the event vocabulary it can speak. Trying to build the model driver first means inventing that vocabulary while also debugging a model, and you will not be able to tell which one is broken.

The cheap way to have it both ways: run the risk-1 experiment — an AI DM in a plain chat window with no product around it — during P0, in parallel. It costs a weekend and answers the question that decides everything else.

08 · Open decisions

Things I could not decide for you, roughly in the order they become blocking.
OQ-1

Product name and domain

"Ember" is a working title I picked to match the aesthetic — it is not researched. Needs a trademark search and a domain before anything is printed or bought.

Blocking: before the marketing homepage goes public. Not before you build it.
OQ-2

Which ruleset, exactly

SRD 5.2.1 is the obvious default and the licensing is clean. But a lighter system means a far smaller rules engine, and the AI DM adjudicates a simpler game more reliably.

Recommendation: SRD 5.2.1 — the audience you're targeting wants this specific game, and a lighter system solves your engineering problem by giving up your market.
OQ-3

How much of the 5e ruleset does the engine actually implement

Full automation of 5e is a multi-year project. Most successful tabletops automate the common 80% and let the DM override the rest.

Recommendation: attacks, saves, damage types, conditions and spell slots automated; everything else is a DM override button. Ship the override before the automation.
OQ-4

Map art: authored, uploaded, or generated

Generated maps are a strong demo and a weak product — inconsistent, slow, hard to make tactically legible. Uploading is what real DMs already do.

Recommendation: upload + a small authored set for alpha. Generation is a P5+ experiment, pre-generated and cached, never live.
OQ-5

Does the table view need to work without the rest of the product

A standalone table view usable by DMs on other platforms would be a genuine wedge — it's the part nobody else has, and it needs almost none of your account system.

Worth considering: a free, no-signup table view could be your entire distribution strategy. It also doubles as the thing you post publicly before the product exists.
OQ-6

Age policy

Needs a decision before any public signup, and it constrains the AI DM's content boundaries.

Recommendation: 13+ at signup for beta, with content boundaries enforced in the DM system prompt and a DM-configurable safety tool set per campaign.

09 · This week

Five concrete things, in order.
  1. Stand up the scaffold. Next.js + Supabase, homepage, signup, login, route shells for /dm, /play, /table. Delivered alongside this document.
  2. Write the schema and the RLS policies together. Not the schema now and security later — the hidden-information rule is far cheaper to build in than to retrofit.
  3. Get one event end-to-end. Two browsers, a button, a Realtime subscription, an event landing on both. Everything in P1 is a variation on this.
  4. Run the risk-1 experiment. One evening, a chat window, a model prompted as a DM, two friends. No product. Answer the only question that matters before you spend a quarter on it.
  5. Decide OQ-2 and OQ-6. Both are cheap to decide now and expensive to change at P3.

What I'd watch out for

The most likely way this goes wrong is not technical. It's that the table view is the most fun part to build, and it will keep pulling you back for one more spell effect while the unglamorous work — sheets, permissions, reconnection — is what actually determines whether anyone can play. The VFX library is finished when fifteen spells look good, not fifty.
